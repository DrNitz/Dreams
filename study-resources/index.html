The subject map, year by year

Philippine medical schools follow the American shape: pre-clinical sciences, then para-clinical sciences, then clinical rotations, then internship. The names and the order vary a little between colleges, so treat this as the common pattern and confirm the exact sequence with your own curriculum.

The usual subject sequence in a Philippine MD programme.
StageSubjectsWhat it feels like
First yearAnatomy, physiology, biochemistry, histology, embryology, medical ethicsVolume. You are memorising a new language and a new body at the same time.
Second yearPathology, pharmacology, microbiology, parasitology, forensic medicine, community medicine, physical diagnosisThe mechanisms click, and the clinical vocabulary starts to make sense.
Third yearMedicine, surgery, paediatrics, obstetrics and gynaecology, radiology, psychiatry, dermatologyWards, clerkships, patient histories, and the first time you present a case.
Fourth yearClinical rotations, specialities, review, internship preparationYou are in the hospital most days. The exam is closer than it feels.
InternshipRotating departments under supervisionLong shifts, real responsibility, and the transition from student to doctor.

Your college may call the years Level 1 to Level 4, or use a different split. The fee sheets from Gullas, DMSF, Jose Maria and Brokenshire use "Level 1" and semesters. That is an administrative label, not a different course.

How the Philippine curriculum is taught

Most colleges use lectures, laboratory sessions and small-group discussions in the pre-clinical years, then ward-based teaching in the clinical years. The lecture is the skeleton. The learning happens in the laboratory, the group work and the past papers.

Two local habits are worth adopting. The first is the "trans" or transcription: students rewrite or summarise lecture material into a shared document. It is efficient, and it is also how a batch builds a common set of notes. The second is recitation: faculty ask questions in class, and you are expected to answer. If you are shy, practice answering aloud before you go.

Attendance rules are stricter than many Indian students expect. A missed laboratory or ward session can cost you the subject, not just the notes. Read the college's attendance policy in the first week, not the week before the exam.

A weekly study system

The students who do well do not study more hours than everyone else. They study with a rhythm. A simple system that survives a five and a half year course:

  1. Preview before the lecture. Ten minutes with the headings is enough. You will recognise the words instead of hearing them for the first time.
  2. Rewrite the lecture the same day. A one-page summary in your own words, not a copy of the slide.
  3. Do questions twice a week. Start with the topics you covered that week, not the whole syllabus.
  4. Keep one review day. Sunday morning for the week's summaries, and nothing new.
  5. Teach one topic to a friend. If you cannot explain it without the notes, you do not know it yet.
A repeatable week, not a timetable you abandon in week two.
DayFocusTime
Monday to FridayPreview, lecture, same-day summary2 to 3 hours outside class

Adjust the hours to your college. The point is the repetition, not the number.

Using Indian resources alongside

Indian textbooks are cheaper, more exam-oriented and closer to the FMGE syllabus. Philippine textbooks are closer to your lectures and your local exams. Use both, but do not try to read both cover to cover.

A practical split: use the Philippine material to pass the college exam, and one Indian review book per subject to keep the FMGE vocabulary alive. For anatomy, physiology, biochemistry, pathology, pharmacology and microbiology, an Indian review book plus your lecture notes is usually enough for the college exam. For community medicine and forensic medicine, the Indian syllabus differs more, so follow your college first and bridge later.

Do not buy a new set of books every year. A good review book, a question bank and your own notes beat a shelf of unopened titles.

FMGE from first year

The FMGE clock starts in first year, whether you like it or not. The exam tests the Indian undergraduate syllabus, and the subjects you learn in first year are the ones you will have forgotten by the time you graduate.

You do not need to study for FMGE every day in first year. You need to keep the Indian thread alive: one review book per subject, a question bank you add to slowly, and a note of the topics your Philippine course does not cover. By the clinical years, that thread is the difference between a six month revision and a two year panic.

Learning on the ward

Clinical years reward a different skill from pre-clinical years. You are not memorising a list. You are learning to ask a question, examine a patient, and present a plan. Three habits that help:

  • Carry a small notebook. Write the case in your own words after you see it. The act of writing is the learning.
  • Present every case you can. Even to a fellow student. The first ten presentations are terrible. The eleventh is better.
  • Ask one question per ward round. Not to show off. To make yourself follow the reasoning.

Foreign students are allowed hands-on work in Philippine hospitals, which is one of the reasons to choose the country. Use that access. Watching is not the same as doing, and the difference shows up in the exam and in your first Indian internship.

Questions

Study questions students ask

Are Philippine medical books different from Indian books?

The content is the same medicine, but the emphasis and the exam style differ. Use Philippine material for your college exams and one Indian review book per subject to keep the FMGE syllabus familiar.

When should I start FMGE preparation?

First year, slowly. One review book per subject and a question bank you add to over time. The students who start in the final year are the ones who run out of time.

Are the notes on this page free?

The study material will be free when it is added. Nothing is downloadable yet, and there is no paid course behind this page.

What is a "trans" in a Philippine medical school?

A transcription, usually a student-made summary of a lecture shared with the batch. It is useful, but it is not a substitute for the lecture or the textbook.

How many hours a day should I study?

Two to three focused hours outside class is a realistic baseline. More matters less than consistency and active recall. A weekly review day is worth more than a late-night cram.

Glossary

The terms your college, the NMC and your seniors use.
How to use this page

If a college email, an NMC notice or a senior uses a term you do not know, find it here. The links go to the page that explains the thing, not just the word.

Terms are grouped by their first letter. Each entry is one or two sentences, because the point is to let you read the next page without stopping.

A

APMC
Association of Philippine Medical Colleges. The association of medical schools in the Philippines, not an Indian regulator.
Apostille
A stamp that authenticates a public document for use in another country. India is part of the Hague Apostille Convention, so many Indian documents are apostilled by the Ministry of External Affairs rather than legalised by an embassy.

B

BSBio
Bachelor of Science in Biology, the pre-medical year some Philippine universities offer. It is not a medical degree and it does not replace NEET.

C

CHED
Commission on Higher Education, the Philippine government body that oversees higher education, including medical schools.
Clerkship
The clinical years of the MD, when students rotate through hospital departments and clerk patients under supervision.
CRMI
Compulsory Rotating Medical Internship. The twelve month internship in India that foreign graduates complete after passing FMGE or NExT.

E

ECFMG
Educational Commission for Foreign Medical Graduates. The body that certifies international medical graduates for US residency applications.

F

FMGE
Foreign Medical Graduate Examination. The screening exam Indian foreign graduates sit before registration. Conducted by NBEMS.
FMGL 2021
The Foreign Medical Graduate Licentiate Regulations, 2021. The NMC rules that apply to students who enrolled on or after 18 November 2021.

I

Internship
The supervised final year of the MD in the Philippines, done in a hospital. Separate from the Indian CRMI.

L

Level
The administrative name some Philippine colleges use for a year of the MD, as in Level 1 or Level 2. It is not a different qualification.

M

MD
Doctor of Medicine. In the Philippines, the primary medical degree, roughly equivalent to MBBS for Indian registration when the college is NMC-listed.
MEA
Ministry of External Affairs, India. It authenticates or apostilles documents for use abroad.

N

NBEMS
National Board of Examinations in Medical Sciences. The body that conducts FMGE, NEET-PG and other postgraduate medical exams in India.
NEET
National Eligibility cum Entrance Test. The Indian entrance exam for medical courses. NMC requires it for Indian students who intend to practise in India.
NEET-PG
The Indian entrance exam for MD, MS and diploma postgraduate seats.
NExT
National Exit Test. The exam being introduced to replace FMGE and unify the final-year and licensing examinations. Check the current status for your batch.
NMC
National Medical Commission. India's medical regulator since 2020, replacing the Medical Council of India.

O

OSCE
Objective Structured Clinical Examination. A practical exam with stations that test history taking, examination and communication.

P

PGI
Postgraduate Institute. A term used loosely for postgraduate medical institutes. Not a qualification by itself.
PRC
Professional Regulation Commission, the Philippine body that licenses professionals, including physicians.

R

Recitation
The classroom habit of being called on to answer questions aloud. Common in Philippine medical schools.
RLE
Related Learning Experience. The supervised clinical practice hours built into Philippine health courses. The term is more common in nursing than in medicine.
Rotation
A block of clinical training in one department, such as medicine, surgery or paediatrics.

S

Semester
Half of an academic year. Philippine colleges often quote fees per semester rather than per year.

T

Trans
A student-made transcription or summary of a lecture, shared within a batch.

U

USMLE
United States Medical Licensing Examination. The exam series for US medical licensure, usually taken alongside ECFMG certification.

W

WDOMS
World Directory of Medical Schools. A directory of medical schools run by WFME and FAIMER. NMC uses it for verification.

9

9F visa
The Philippine student visa category. Many students enter on a temporary visa and convert to 9F after enrolment, so confirm the current process with the college and embassy.
