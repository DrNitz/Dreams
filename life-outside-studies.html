The rest of the week

Life outside studies in the Philippines

The degree is the reason you are there. The week is bigger than the degree. This is the part between exam blocks: food, weekends, the sea, the mountains, and the friends who make five years feel shorter.

Reviewed 25 November 2026. First hand from Cebu.

The week has a shape beyond the ward

Medical school in the Philippines runs on blocks. A term of lectures and practicals, then examinations, then a short gap before the next term. The gap is not a holiday. It is the only week in the calendar when you can sleep, see a doctor, fix your visa paperwork and still have two days left for something that is not medicine.

I learned to protect that week. Not because I was disciplined, but because the students who never stopped working were the ones who burned out in second year. The ones who lasted had a life outside the college. It was usually a small one: a favourite canteen, a weekend walk, a group that played basketball on Sunday. Small was enough.

Food is the first thing you build

You will eat out more than you did at home, at least in the first year, because the hostel kitchen is shared and the timetable is not friendly. That makes food the first part of your life outside studies, whether you planned it or not.

The practical version: find one place near campus that will cook rice and egg for you at any hour, and one place that serves vegetables. Then find one that is cheap enough to eat at twice a week. Learn to cook three things badly. Three is enough to stop the takeaway bill from eating your monthly budget.

Filipino food is not Indian food. It is rice-based, often sweet, often pork, and rarely as spicy as you expect. The students who settle fastest stop looking for dal and start eating what the city eats, then keep Indian food for the days they need it. Cebu has spice shops and Indian restaurants if you know where to look. Manila has more. Davao has fewer, and the students there learn to cook sooner.

Weekends: the sea, the mountains, or the mall

Cebu gives you three kinds of weekend. The first is the sea. Mactan is a short ride from the city, and ferries from the port go to Bohol and other islands. The second is the mountains, which are cooler and cheaper than the coast and a better cure for a bad week. The third is the mall, which sounds like a failure of imagination until you remember that air conditioning, cheap food and a cinema are a legitimate way to spend a Sunday in a hot country.

Manila is a different weekend. The city is larger, the traffic is a tax on every plan, and the good parts are spread out. Students there tend to pick one area and stay in it: a market, a park, a church, a mall. The students who try to see everything in one weekend spend most of it in a jeepney.

Davao is quieter and closer to the water and the mountains at the same time. It is the city where a weekend trip feels like a trip rather than a commute. It is also the city where you notice the smallness fastest, which is either the point or the problem, depending on you.

Festivals, and the one week the city stops

Every city has its festival. Cebu's is Sinulog, in January, and it is the week the city stops being ordinary. Streets close, the campus empties, and every student who can afford a ticket is either in the parade or watching it. It is loud, crowded and worth doing once. It is also the week to lock your door and keep your phone in your front pocket.

The smaller festivals are better in some ways. A barangay fiesta, a college intramural, a friend's birthday that turns into a whole afternoon. Those are the days you remember when the degree is over, not the exam you passed by two marks.

Sport, gyms and the cheap version of both

Basketball is the national sport and the easiest way to meet people who are not in your batch. Every barangay has a court, and a pick-up game is usually open to a foreign student who asks. If you are not a basketball player, walk. The city is walkable in the parts that matter, and walking is the cheapest way to learn a place.

Gyms near campus are cheap by Indian city standards, and they are also where a lot of students disappear for an hour a day. That hour is not vanity. It is the part of the week when nobody is asking you to memorise anything.

Travel on a student budget

The Philippines is a country of islands, and the cheapest travel is the trip you take with people who already know the route. A group of four splits a van, a boat and a room. A group of one pays for all of it. If you want to see the country, join the group.

Book early, travel light, and avoid the weeks when everyone else is travelling. The cost of a trip is mostly transport and accommodation, and both double when the whole country has the same idea. A long weekend in a nearby province can cost less than a month of eating out, if you plan it that way.

One warning: do not travel in the week before a major exam. The trip will be in your head the whole time, and the exam will not care that you saw a waterfall.

Friends, and the ones who are not your batch

The friends who make five years feel shorter are usually not the ones you meet in the first week. They are the ones you meet in the second year, in a hospital corridor, in a group chat about a subject nobody understands, or in a canteen at eleven at night. They are Filipino students, Indian seniors, a landlord's nephew, the person who fixes your laptop.

My advice is the same as the advice about food. Build a small life outside the college, and keep it small enough to maintain. A handful of people, a place to eat, a Sunday routine. That is enough to make a foreign city feel like somewhere you live rather than somewhere you are waiting to leave.

The balance is the whole skill

Nobody gets this right in the first year. You will either work too much or too little, and you will find out which in the first set of examinations. The correction is not a new timetable. It is a smaller one: one evening a week that is not study, one weekend a month that is not catch-up, one person you can call when the week has gone badly.

The degree is five and a half years. You cannot white-knuckle it. You have to live in it.
